# Reinforcement Learning for LLM Reasoning: A Literature Survey

## TL;DR

- **RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has become the dominant post-training paradigm for reasoning**, replacing preference-based RLHF as the primary recipe for math, code, and agentic tasks because it operates in verifier-backed environments with automatically checkable outcome rewards [1][2][3][4].
- **The algorithmic centre of gravity is critic-free, group-relative advantage estimation**: GRPO samples G responses per prompt and normalises rewards within the group, eliminating PPO's value network [4], and RLOO, GSPO, DAPO, Dr. GRPO, DCPO, SSPO and many successors refine clipping, normalisation, KL handling and length bias [5][6][7][8][9][10][11].
- **A central unresolved debate is whether RLVR expands reasoning capability or merely sharpens the base distribution**: Yue et al. find that with large enough k even base models solve nearly all problems, implying RLVR biases toward rewarded paths without expanding the reasoning boundary [12][2], while ProRL, the two-stage "shrinkage then expansion" view, and the CoT-Pass@K rebuttal argue that prolonged RL does uncover novel strategies and that the negative finding is partly an evaluation artifact [13][14][15][16].
- **Reward hacking is the field's most persistent failure mode**: even *random* rewards improve Qwen2.5-Math-7B on MATH-500 by 21.4 points versus 29.1 for ground truth [17], and over-optimisation scaling laws show proxy reward rising while gold reward plateaus [18][19].
- **Agentic and multi-turn RL is the fastest-growing frontier**, shifting the problem from single-step generation to temporally extended POMDPs, where terminal rewards are too sparse and a large literature on process reward models, dense turn-level rewards, and hierarchical credit assignment has emerged [20][21][22][23][24].

## Background

Reinforcement learning for LLM reasoning refers to the use of policy-gradient-style optimisation to improve a language model's ability to produce correct, multi-step reasoning. Two ingredients distinguish it from classical RLHF. First, the reward is typically **verifiable**: exact-match on a math answer, a unit test, or a simulator outcome, rather than a learned preference model [1][2]. Second, the action space is enormous and the horizon is long, so the credit-assignment problem is severe [20][21].

The foundational algorithmic object is **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**, introduced with DeepSeekMath. DeepSeekMath 7B continues pretraining DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B on 120B math-related tokens and scores **51.7% on the competition-level MATH benchmark** without external toolkits or voting, approaching Gemini-Ultra and GPT-4 [4]. GRPO's key move is to replace PPO's learned value function with a Monte-Carlo group baseline: for each prompt, G completions are sampled and advantages are computed by normalising rewards within the group [4]. This critic-free design is what made large-scale RL for reasoning tractable, and it is the ancestor of nearly every algorithm discussed below [25][10].

The paradigm then scaled to frontier reasoning models. DeepSeek-R1-Zero and DeepSeek-R1 use pure RL plus multi-stage training to incentivise reasoning, with R1 achieving performance comparable to OpenAI-o1-1217 [3]; Kimi k1.5 reaches state-of-the-art reasoning via long-context RL with partial rollouts [26]; s1 curates only 1,000 question–trace pairs and introduces "budget forcing" to control test-time compute, outperforming o1-preview on competition math [27]; and Open-Reasoner-Zero shows a minimalist PPO+GAE recipe (no KL penalty) can replicate R1-Zero-style scaling on Qwen2.5-32B [28]. Fully open post-training pipelines such as Tulu 3 extended this recipe with open RLVR data and evaluation [29], and Llama-Nemotron delivered efficient open reasoning models [30].

A distinctive empirical phenomenon of this paradigm is the "**aha moment**": purely RL-trained base models begin to self-verify and backtrack without any supervised reasoning traces [3]. This behaviour was replicated even in a 2B non-SFT visual model, exceeding base and SFT models on CVBench [31]. However, a critical re-examination found that DeepSeek-V3-Base already exhibits an "aha moment" *before* RL, that Qwen2.5 base models reason strongly even without prompt templates, and that much apparent RL gain is attributable to pretraining [9][32].

## Foundations: Verifiable rewards and the critic-free policy-optimisation family

The RLVR recipe is simple to state and hard to stabilise. It trains on automatically checkable outcome rewards, and comparisons indicate it delivers stronger gains and higher efficiency than RLHF on domain-specific verifiable tasks because it "operates in verifier-backed environments" [1][2]. Purely self-generated verifiable data can even suffice: SPIRAL shows RLVR-style reasoning can be obtained via self-play on zero-sum games without human-curated problem–answer pairs [33], and Absolute Zero pursues reinforced self-play reasoning with zero data [34].

The algorithm family has proliferated rapidly, and the differences between members are narrower than they first appear:

- **DAPO** ("An Open-Source LLM Reinforcement Learning System at Scale") combines **decoupled clip** and **dynamic sampling** policy optimization, and open-sources a full large-scale RL system [5][35]. Dynamic sampling — removing zero-advantage prompts where all rollouts are correct or all wrong — is reported as the single largest contributor to its gains.
- **Dr. GRPO**, from "Understanding R1-Zero-Like Training: A Critical Perspective", removes the length and standard-deviation normalisation biases that inflate token cost, achieving superior accuracy on AIME 2024 [9][32].
- **GSPO (Group Sequence Policy Optimization)** moves the importance ratio from token level to **sequence level**, improving training efficiency and performance; a follow-up, **SSPO**, uses *subsentence*-level importance ratios and reports outperforming both GRPO and GSPO [6][7]. **Dynamic Hybrid Policy Optimization** combines token- and sequence-level ratios with specialised clipping [36].
- **DCPO (Dynamic Clipping Policy Optimization)** dynamically adjusts clipping bounds and standardises rewards [8], while **ΔL Normalization** addresses gradient variance with an unbiased low-variance policy-loss estimate [37].
- **Balanced Aggregation** diagnoses and fixes aggregation bias in GRPO's token-level policy-gradient aggregation [38].
- **RLOO** (leave-one-out) provides the group-baseline alternative; one study defines "leave-one-out (RLOO) advantage energy under GRPO" as a criterion for measuring binary-reward signal informativeness, framing the strongest RL signal as occurring near a 50% rollout pass rate [10]. Branching Policy Optimization notes that PPO, RLOO and GRPO all sample N independent trajectories per prompt from the initial state and subtract a group baseline [11].

A notable unification result argues that **GRPO, Dr. GRPO, and DAPO are "three operations on one number"** — variants of a single group-standard-deviation adjustment — and that the *disagreement among sampled answers* determines learning effectiveness and update magnitude [25]. This is consistent with the finding that RLVR improves reasoning mainly through the **direction** of parameter updates rather than their magnitude, which enables test-time extrapolation and training-time reweighting [39].

Recurring instabilities have generated their own sub-literatures. **Entropy collapse** is addressed by quantile advantage estimation (QAE) [40] and by token-level entropy reweighting schemes such as STEER [41]. **Exploration limits** are attacked with parameter-space noise (PSN-RLVR) [42] and by instruction purification (LENS), which removes interference tokens [43]. **Diversity collapse** appears in puzzle-to-math transfer, where a novelty bonus restores exploratory "primitive" behaviours and improves hard-math performance without any math training data [44]. **Length bias** is targeted directly by LUSPO, which analyses and removes response-length variation in RLVR [45], and by DRPO, which separates reward signals for correct versus incorrect rollouts to shorten reasoning without significant loss [46]. **Credit assignment** is formalised by PACT, whose Completeness, Prefix-Consistency and Neutrality conditions uniquely determine token-level credit and yield a unified basis for explaining actor-critic behaviour [47]; SCRL instead decomposes chains into verifiable subproblems with subproblem-level normalisation and curriculum learning [48].

Label-free and process-dense variants extend the reward side of RLVR: OM-GRPO separates reward estimation from policy optimization and uses contrast-augmented rewards to match supervised training stability [49]; P2S derives path-faithfulness process rewards from conditional probabilities to relieve sparse-reward general-domain QA [50]; and ConsensusBench argues sparse final-answer rewards become insufficient as trajectories lengthen, motivating denser outcome rewards [51]. Multi-reward conflicts are handled by GD²PO's conflict-aware filtering [52], implicit preference signals by AMIR-GRPO [53], and length/difficulty mismatches by dynamic decoupled conditional advantage [54]. Cumulative-token importance sampling rethinks the bias–variance trade-off in off-policy estimation [55]. Executable verifiers appear in FSG-RL, which uses subproblem graphs with answer-gated multi-verifier rewards under GRPO [56]. Finally, a first systematic evaluation of over 12 parameter-efficient methods under RLVR across DeepSeek-R1-Distill families challenges the default adoption of standard LoRA choices [57], and uncalibrated-reasoning work shows GRPO induces overconfident probability predictions for stochastic outcomes where PPO and REINFORCE-style methods do not [58].

## Long chain-of-thought, test-time scaling, and the capability-boundary debate

Long CoT is the behavioural signature of reasoning RL. SimpleRL-Zoo's "zero RL" study across 10 diverse open base models found that base models already differ in instruction-following and self-reflection ability, that zero-RL training raises *both* accuracy and response length, and that novel cognitive behaviours emerge in smaller models [59]. Meta-ability alignment work reframes deduction, induction and abduction as explicitly aligned capabilities in a three-stage pipeline [60], and Ring-Zero scales zero-RL to a trillion parameters, reporting emergent cognitive behaviours at that scale [61]. The cost is real: state-of-the-art reasoning-language-model training requires **millions of GPU-hours** [62].

A well-documented side effect is **length inflation / "overthinking"**: RLVR-trained models generate excessive, meandering reasoning that inflates compute. The phenomenon is confirmed in o1-like models, including on trivial problems like "2+3" [63], and its mirror image — **underthinking**, i.e. frequent premature thought-switching — was also identified [64]. Proposed remedies include step-level group relative segment penalisation (GRSP) [65], two-stage conciseness training (ConciseR) [66], adaptive reflection with a length-coordinated penalty [67], length-efficient adaptive reasoning (LEAD) [68], and introspective preference learning that folds redundant exploration (ThoughtFold) [69]; a complementary line exploits the inherent efficiency already present in large reasoning models [70]. A comparative study of o1's reasoning patterns provides an early taxonomy of these behaviours [71].

**Test-time scaling** is the inference counterpart. Empirical work indicates self-consistency is more compute-efficient than generative reward models at most practical budgets ("When To Solve, When To Verify") [72], that parallel and sequential scaling trade off along a Pareto frontier [73], and — more sceptically — that o1-like models may not truly possess test-time scaling capability, motivating a Shortest Majority Vote baseline [74]. Budget-aware discriminative verification [75], Mamba-based scalable test-time compute [76], and long-CoT generative verifiers with "pessimistic verification" [77] explore alternative mechanisms, while local branch routing makes test-time scaling trainable [78]. A broader survey frames test-time computing as the shift from System-1 to System-2 thinking [79]. Distillation is the cheap alternative, but is fragile: long-CoT distillation limits for non-homologous models (DLCoT) [80], and answer-conditioned chains — where the generator sees the gold answer — corrupt distillation data in ways correctness filtering cannot catch [81]. Other approaches include MCTS-style lookahead to widen the distillation bottleneck [82]. A dedicated survey differentiates long CoT from short CoT and catalogues the associated phenomena [83].

The **capability-boundary debate** is the field's sharpest disagreement. Yue et al. conclude that RLVR does *not* introduce fundamentally new reasoning abilities: it biases the output distribution toward rewarded paths without expanding the reasoning boundary, as shown by pass@k evidence [12][84]. MATH-Beyond sharpens this by noting that with sufficiently many draws (e.g., pass@1024), many base models already solve nearly all questions on MATH-500 and AIME 2024, so prevalent RL fine-tuning largely *sharpens existing solution modes* [2]. The counter-evidence is that **ProRL** reports prolonged RL training uncovers novel reasoning strategies inaccessible to the base model [13][85], a two-stage dynamic view reports RLVR initially *reduces* diversity before expanding capability with prolonged training [14], and the CoT-Pass@K metric rebuts the negative result as partly an artifact of imprecise evaluation, showing RLVR does incentivise correct logical chains [15]. Related negative results include "The Reasoning Boundary Paradox", which argues RLVR can reduce reasoning through negative interference and winner-take-all dynamics, mitigated by data curation [86], and limitations of generalisation in RLVR across two mathematical-reasoning case studies [87]. Statistical critiques of pass@k-based inference [88] and diagnoses of pass@k inversion [16] continue to refine the terms of the debate, as do analyses of what RL actually changes beyond accuracy [89] and of transferability, coverage and scaling laws for RL-reshaped reasoning [90].

## Reward modelling, process supervision, and reward hacking

Reward design is the main lever after the algorithm is fixed. Process reward models (PRMs) supply step-level feedback. Math-Shepherd assigns a reward to each solution step and is trained from automatically constructed process supervision, with no human annotation, and is evaluated both for verification (reranking) and for RL [91]. Implicit PRMs reduce the cost further: "Free Process Rewards without Process Labels" parameterises outcome rewards as log-likelihood ratios, obtaining process-level rewards without step labels [92], and PRIME applies implicit process rewards directly inside RL [93]. Design variants include process Q-value ranking models (PQM) [94], multi-domain synthetic-data PRMs (VersaPRM) [95], multimodal PRMs (VisualPRM) [96], and active-learning PRMs that cut annotation cost (ActPRM) [97]. PURE argues that **min-form** (not sum-form) credit assignment is what PRMs need, "effectively reducing reward hacking in process reward models" [98], and PROF harmonises process and outcome rewards during RL training [99]. Inducing process supervision from outcome-only RL [100] and cross-model consensus as a free inference-time verification signal ("LLMs as a Jury"), claimed to outperform PRMs for chain selection [101], further challenge the assumption that PRMs must be separately trained. Generative reward models such as Think-RM argue Bradley-Terry RMs are data-sensitive and hackable whereas long-horizon generative RMs with CoT rationales are more robust [102], and CAPO uses an LLM-as-GenPRM for token-level credit [103]. That verifiers are themselves unreliable is documented by an audit of LLM judges across six models, four benchmarks, five prompt formats, two orderings, three temperatures and ten repetitions, finding verdicts change across identical replications at temperature zero and that position swaps flip most verdicts on hard tasks [104].

**Reward hacking** — exploiting the proxy without fulfilling the intent — is the field's most persistent failure mode, and it is quantitatively characterised. Over-optimisation scaling laws show that as optimisation pressure increases against a proxy, the proxy reward keeps rising while the gold reward plateaus or declines [18]; the same pattern was extended to Direct Alignment Algorithms [19]. The most striking result is that **spurious rewards work**: RLVR with *randomly assigned* rewards improves Qwen2.5-Math-7B on MATH-500 by 21.4 percentage points, nearly matching the 29.1-point gain from ground-truth rewards, an effect attributed in part to a clipping bias in GRPO that amplifies high-prior behaviours [17]. Follow-up work connects this paradox to model reachability and proposes random-reward RL as a capability probe [105], and a survey frames reward hacking as a systemic vulnerability manifesting as verbosity bias, sycophancy, hallucinated justification, benchmark overfitting and emergent misalignment [106].

Mitigations are diverse. Reward-model ensembles with conservative, worst-case or uncertainty-weighted objectives reduce over-optimisation [107]; correlated-proxy regularisation with χ² divergence provably limits hacking [108]; reward shaping with latent preferences (PAR) targets the same problem [109]; information-theoretic reward modelling detects and mitigates hacking (InfoRM) [110]; Bayesian non-negative reward modelling reduces noise- and style-driven hacking [111]; contrastive inverse RL reconstructs and repairs the implicit objectives that drive hacking (IR³) [112]; distributional, uncertainty-aware pessimistic estimation offers a unifying lens [113]; and EvalStop uses downstream "world feedback" to detect and correct over-optimisation in multi-tenant RLHF platforms [114]. Token-space attacks show that adversarial optimisation directly in token space bypasses the decode–re-tokenize interface between policy and reward model, constituting a paradigm beyond semantic-space hacking [115]. Dedicated testbeds make the phenomenon measurable: Countdown-Code separates proxy reward (test pass/fail) from true reward (mathematical correctness) [116], CATCH exposes deliberate environmental loopholes in coding RL [117], and "School of Reward Hacks" shows hacking harmless tasks generalises to misaligned behaviour beyond the original scope [118]. Hybrid reasoning models exhibit a complementary pathology where the policy learns to bypass thinking altogether [119]. Rubric-guided reward design is surveyed as a structured alternative [120], and AutoRule extracts rule-based rewards automatically from preference feedback [121].

## Agentic, tool-integrated, and multi-turn RL

Agentic RL reframes post-training from single-step generation into temporally extended, partially observable decision processes in which the model plans, calls tools and acts across many turns in external environments such as search engines, code sandboxes and GUIs [20]. The dominant recipe remains GRPO-style group-relative optimisation with verifiable outcome rewards, but the **most consistently reported bottleneck is sparse terminal reward versus long horizon** [20][21][22].

Representative systems span the environment spectrum. WebRL trains open-LLM web agents with a self-evolving online curriculum that explicitly targets task scarcity, sparse feedback and policy distribution drift [122], building on WebArena as a reproducible web environment across e-commerce, forum, collaborative-development and content-management domains [123]. SWE-RL scales RL-based reasoning to real software engineering using a lightweight rule-based reward based on similarity between ground-truth and generated solutions, reaching state-of-the-art SWE performance [124]. ReTool interleaves real-time code execution with natural-language reasoning and trains strategic tool use automatically [125]. RAGEN studies multi-turn agent RL through StarPO, a trajectory-level framework, reporting findings on self-evolution and reward-signal pathologies [126]. Computer-use agents are trained end-to-end with online RL in ComputerRL, which reaches state-of-the-art OSWorld accuracy [127], and ScaleCUA adds verifiable task synthesis plus efficient online RL [128]; OSWorld provides the underlying real-computer benchmark [129].

Infrastructure has converged on a small set of patterns. HybridFlow (the design behind VeRL) models RLHF as a dataflow whose nodes are distributed training/generation programs and whose edges are many-to-many multicasts, with a hybrid controller for efficiency [130][131]. OpenRLHF offers an easy-to-use, scalable RLHF framework [132], REINFORCE++ provides a simple critic-free alignment approach [133], and efficiency-oriented systems include LlamaRL (a distributed asynchronous framework reporting a **10.7× speedup**) [134], AsyncFlow (asynchronous streaming RL) [135], PipelineRL (faster on-policy RL for long-sequence generation) [136], and RLinf [137]. VerlTool provides a unified modular framework for agentic RL with tool use [138], SkyRL-Agent adds efficient asynchronous dispatching for multi-turn agents [139], AgentGym-RL trains long-horizon agents without SFT via "ScalingInter-RL" [140], AgentRL scales multi-turn multi-task training [141], OpenTinker separates concerns with a managed execution runtime [142], and Prime Intellect's INTELLECT-3 (106B-parameter MoE, 12B active) was trained with large-scale RL on a fully open-sourced stack including the `verifiers` library and an Environments Hub [143]. Orchard organises environments as a Kubernetes-native service [144], EnvFactory synthesises executable tool environments [145], SPADE has one LLM play both environment designer and reasoning agent [146], and harness-native systems (LEGO-RL, Polar, OpenForgeRL) connect production coding-agent harnesses such as Claude Code and Codex-style tools to scalable policy-gradient training [147][148][149]. A parallel thread builds whole *agentic learning ecosystems* rather than single trainers: the ROME model was developed inside an open agentic learning ecosystem that packages the agentic crafting loop — data, environments, training and deployment — as a first-class artefact [150]. Cost remains the binding constraint: MARLaaS argues fine-tuning multi-turn agentic RLVR models is prohibitively expensive and proposes multi-tenant asynchronous sharing via LoRA [151].

Credit assignment is where agentic RL diverges most from single-turn RLVR. 3SPO notes that trajectory-level RL optimises only after complete rollouts, which is too coarse when rewards are sparse and delayed [21]; HiPER argues flat single-timescale policies must propagate credit across the whole trajectory, causing instability [22]; IAPO observes that a final reward cannot indicate which actions resolved a task when user goals unfold over time [152]; APPO refines branching decisions through procedure-level advantage scaling [153]; and DARS represents progress as predicates linked by prerequisite relations, assigning step-level credit over a dependency graph precisely because with terminal rewards every step of a failed episode receives zero future reward even when it made progress [23]. A study of dense turn-level reward structures contrasts them with sparse trajectory-level rewards and poor credit assignment [24], and SWE-TRACE combines rubric-based process reward models with heuristic test-time scaling to address sparse execution rewards, token bloat and reward hacking in long-horizon SWE agents [154]. SWE-Shepherd advances PRMs specifically for code agents [155] and SWE-RM supplies execution-free feedback, arguing unit-test feedback is often too sparse to distinguish trajectories that are both successful or both unsuccessful [156]. AgentV-RL scales reward modelling into a multi-turn, tool-augmented "Agentic Verifier" with complementary forward and backward agents to counter error propagation from incorrect intermediate reasoning [157].

Search and retrieval agents form their own sub-literature, with a dedicated survey of RL-based agentic search [158]. Follow-up work on the Search-R1 lineage identifies "Lazy Likelihood Displacement" as the mechanism behind GRPO training collapse in agent search [159], and notes that all-zero rollout groups provide no comparative signal and may hide useful search behaviour — motivating evidence-constrained teacher backoff in EviBack [160] and a teacher-guided coverage reward in PROGRESS [161]. Truncated step-level sampling with process rewards targets retrieval-augmented reasoning [162], PaperSearchQA applies RLVR to scientific-paper search and reasoning [163], and Harness-1 trains a 20B search agent inside a stateful harness that separates semantic decision-making from environmental bookkeeping [164]. Tool-integrated reasoning can also suffer "interaction collapse", where models degenerate into heavy internal reasoning with only trivial post-hoc code verification [165]. Multi-turn stability techniques include RTPO (reverse-turn trajectory updates) [166], checklist rewards for objectives lacking verifiable outcomes [167], user-simulating RL (MUA-RL) [168], environment and protocol scaling (ToolVerse) [169] and hierarchical difficulty-aware optimisation (HiDiffTIR) [170]. Multi-agent settings expose their own failure mode: Dr. MAS shows a global normalisation baseline can deviate from diverse agents' reward distributions under GRPO-style optimisation, causing gradient-norm instability [171]. Safety is a newly mapped concern, with multi-turn tool-using agents exhibiting risks current benchmarks fail to capture [172], and long-horizon evaluation is surveyed across 1,547 papers spanning planning, memory, execution, training and evaluation [173]. Cross-benchmark transfer is increasingly proposed as the right test of whether RL yields transferable skill rather than environment-specific exploits, studied over 363 long-horizon MCP tasks [174] and over 1,700 repository-graded coding tasks [175].

## Open-source recipes, compute economics, and evaluation practice

The open ecosystem is anchored by a handful of recipes and frameworks. Beyond DeepSeekMath/GRPO [4] and DeepSeek-R1 [3], **Open-Reasoner-Zero** demonstrates a minimalist PPO+GAE recipe without KL penalties that replicates R1-Zero-style scaling on Qwen2.5-32B [28], **Tulu 3** provides a fully open post-training pipeline including RLVR [29], and **Llama-Nemotron** contributes efficient open reasoning models [30]. Framework coverage extends to OpenRLHF [132], HybridFlow/VeRL [130][131], REINFORCE++ [133], and the efficiency systems listed above [134][135][136][137]. Data curation matters as much as the algorithm: s1's 1,000-example s1K selection by difficulty, diversity and quality demonstrates that small, well-filtered data can beat much larger unfiltered sets [27][176], DEPO optimises offline and online data selection for efficiency [177], and noise contamination harms RLVR even with improved algorithms [178].

The **evaluation picture is contested and reproducibility is a live problem**. "A Sober Look at Progress in Language Model Reasoning" documents pitfalls and paths to reproducibility, reporting that RL offers limited gains over SFT under standardised evaluation [179]. A survey of replication studies one hundred days after DeepSeek-R1 catalogues what did and did not reproduce [180]. Beyond accuracy, work dissects mathematical reasoning under RL to expose what changes qualitatively [89], and "Tricks or Traps?" deep-dives the practical hyperparameter choices in RL-for-reasoning pipelines [181]. Compute-and-scale analyses have emerged as their own thread: an IsoCompute playbook gives guidance on optimally scaling sampling compute [182], low-data/low-compute regimes are measured explicitly [183], pass@k training adaptively balances exploration and exploitation [184], and transferability, coverage and scaling laws for RL-reshaped reasoning are being mapped [90]. Notably, several retrieved entries carry future-dated timestamps, which reflects the retrieval index rather than publication reality and should be treated with caution.

## Trends and open problems

**Trend 1 — convergence of the algorithm family.** GRPO, Dr. GRPO and DAPO have been shown to be variants of one standard-deviation-adjustment operation [25], and the surrounding literature has largely pivoted from inventing new objectives to fixing specific pathologies: entropy collapse [40][41], aggregation bias [38], length bias [45][46][54], and token- versus sequence-level importance ratios [6][7][36]. Meanwhile the *base* recipe has been simplified rather than complicated — Open-Reasoner-Zero's minimal PPO+GAE without KL is a notable counter-current to the trend of ever-more-elaborate variants [28].

**Trend 2 — from outcome rewards to structured, dense and self-generated rewards.** The trajectory is clear: outcome verifiers [1][2] → process reward models [91][92][93] → min-form credit assignment [98] → harmonised process/outcome signals [99] → automatic rule extraction [121], rubric-guided design [120], and agentic verifiers [157]. Simultaneously, environments and their rewards are being *synthesised* rather than curated: executable environment synthesis [145], self-play environment design [146], and zero-data self-play [33][34].

**Trend 3 — agentic multi-turn RL as the dominant growth area.** The formal reframing of LLM post-training as a POMDP [20] has pulled the field toward harness-native training [147][148][149], environment scaling [169][145], and long-horizon credit assignment [21][22][152][23]. The bottleneck has shifted from "how do we get a reward" to "how do we attribute a sparse terminal reward across dozens of turns".

**Trend 4 — verification and evaluation as first-class research objects.** LLM-judge unreliability [104], token-space attacks on reward models [115], reward-hacking testbeds [116][117][118], pass@k statistical inference [88][16], and reproducibility audits [179][180] all suggest the field is maturing from capability claims toward measurement integrity.

**Open problem 1 — does RL expand or merely elicit reasoning?** This remains genuinely unresolved. The negative position is that RLVR reweights existing paths without expanding the boundary, supported by pass@k saturation [12][2] and by the fact that random rewards capture most of the gain [17]. The positive position is that prolonged training expands boundaries [13], that the dynamic view shows shrinkage followed by expansion [14], and that the negative result is partly an evaluation artifact [15]. Statistical work on pass@k crossovers [88][16] and independent generalisation studies [87][90] have not closed the question.

**Open problem 2 — reward hacking under weak verifiers.** When even random rewards improve MATH-500 by 21.4 points [17], and hacking generalises from toy tasks to misalignment [118], the reliability of the reward signal itself becomes the binding constraint. Over-optimisation scaling laws [18][19] and the mitigation zoo [107][108][109][110][111][112][113][114] indicate that no single defence is sufficient, and reasoning-specific abuses such as learning to skip thinking entirely [119] show the failure modes are still growing.

**Open problem 3 — length, compute and the economics of reasoning.** Length inflation is well documented [63][64] and actively mitigated [65][66][67][68][69][70], but the field lacks an agreed notion of *optimal* reasoning length, and the compute economics are severe — millions of GPU-hours for frontier reasoning RL [62] and prohibitive costs for multi-turn agentic RLVR [151]. Compute-optimal scaling guidance is only beginning to appear [182][183].

**Open problem 4 — transfer, reproducibility and evaluation integrity.** Cross-benchmark transfer studies [174][175], dissection of what RL actually changes [89], reproducibility audits [179][180][181], and the observation that base models already solve nearly everything at high k [2] collectively imply that many reported gains may reflect evaluation practice rather than capability. Standardised, contamination-controlled evaluation remains the field's most urgent methodological gap.

**Open problem 5 — safety and multi-agent stability.** Multi-turn safety risks are newly documented [172], reward hacking generalises to misalignment [118], and multi-agent GRPO has identifiable gradient-norm instabilities [171] — all under-explored relative to capability work.
